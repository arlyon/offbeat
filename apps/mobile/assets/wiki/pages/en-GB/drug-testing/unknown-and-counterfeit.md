# Unknown substances, counterfeit products and opioid contamination

> **Treat an unknown product as unknown.** Packaging, branding, tablet colour, smell, taste, a seller's claim, effects and a home-test result cannot establish identity, strength or safety.

## Unknown powders, liquids and pills

Do not taste, sniff or try a small amount to identify a product. Effects overlap between drugs, may be delayed, and may reflect a mixture or poisoning rather than the expected substance.

Keep the material sealed and separate from known substances, medicines, food and drink. Avoid spreading powder or touching your face. If safe, retain packaging, containers, photographs and a sample for medical staff or an eligible professional testing service. Do not handle material in a way that puts you or others at risk.

If exposure may have happened, a material test is not medical triage. Symptoms can develop later. Seek urgent clinical advice rather than waiting for an online or laboratory result.

## Unknown vapes and edibles

A branded or familiar-looking vape, refill or edible does not prove that it contains THC, CBD, HHC or cannabis. Unregulated products may contain different cannabinoids, synthetic cannabinoids, several substances or contaminants, and ingredients can vary between units sold together. Colour, flavour, packaging and an effect that resembles cannabis cannot establish identity.

Home testing cannot exclude every synthetic cannabinoid or make an unregulated vape safe. Collapse, seizure, severe agitation, chest pain, unconsciousness or abnormal breathing requires immediate emergency action. See [Cannabis](wiki:substances.cannabis) and [Synthetic cannabinoids, Spice and SCRAs](wiki:substances.synthetic-cannabinoids-and-spice).

## Counterfeit medicines

A tablet or blister that resembles a prescription medicine may contain a different drug, an unexpected amount, several drugs or no stated medicine. This includes products sold as painkillers, anti-anxiety medicines, sleeping tablets and ADHD medicines.

Do not rely on a logo, imprint, packaging or online photograph. Medicines from an unregulated source should not be treated as equivalent to a pharmacy-supplied product. A single analysed tablet does not prove that other tablets in the pack or batch match it.

## Synthetic opioids and nitazenes

Potent synthetic opioids, including nitazenes and fentanyl, can suppress breathing. They may appear in products sold as heroin or other drugs, and have also been found in counterfeit medicines. The UK drug supply changes, so an old alert or a negative strip cannot rule out current contamination.

Opioid and nitazene test strips are limited, product-specific screens. They may miss new analogues, material below the detection threshold or contamination outside the tested portion. They can also cross-react. A result cannot show quantity or make use safe. See [Professional and at-home drug checking](wiki:drug-testing.professional-and-at-home).

## Act on signs, not certainty

Abnormal, slow or stopped breathing, blue or grey lips or skin, pinpoint pupils, snoring or gurgling sounds, limpness, collapse, unconsciousness or inability to wake someone may indicate opioid poisoning or another emergency.

Follow [Get emergency help](wiki:emergency.get-help) immediately. Do not wait for certainty, a test result or a response to naloxone. That guide covers calling 999 or 112, checking breathing, CPR and the recovery position.

If naloxone is available, give it according to the supplied product instructions or your training while emergency help is being called. Naloxone only reverses opioid effects, can wear off before the opioid, and does not replace airway support, CPR, observation or an ambulance. Tell responders what was given and when.

Do not make the person vomit, give them food, drink or stimulants, put them in a shower, or leave them to sleep. Preserve packaging or a sample for responders only when it is safe.
