# Opioids and potent synthetic opioids

> **CALL 999 OR 112 NOW** if someone is unconscious, cannot be woken, has slow, irregular or stopped breathing, has blue or grey lips or skin, makes snoring or gurgling sounds, is limp, has collapsed or is rapidly getting worse. **Do not wait for certainty or for naloxone to work.** Follow [Get emergency help](wiki:emergency.get-help).

## Act now: breathing and naloxone

1. Call 999 or 112 and say the person is unresponsive or not breathing normally. Send someone for festival medical staff and an AED. Tell the call handler that opioid exposure is possible.
2. Open the airway and check breathing. If they are not breathing normally, start CPR immediately and follow the call handler. If they are unconscious but breathing normally, use the recovery position and keep checking.
3. If naloxone is available, give it promptly according to the supplied product instructions or your training. Anyone can administer available naloxone to save a life in an emergency.
4. Continue breathing support or CPR as directed. If there is no adequate response, further naloxone may be needed, especially with a potent synthetic opioid; follow the product instructions and call handler rather than improvising a dose.
5. Stay until the ambulance arrives and keep watching their breathing. Even if the person wakes, naloxone can wear off before the opioid and breathing can slow again. Encourage them to remain for ambulance assessment, do not leave them alone, and tell responders if they try to leave or take more opioids.

Naloxone reverses opioid effects only. It does not reverse alcohol, benzodiazepines, GHB/GBL, pregabalin, gabapentin, ketamine or xylazine. Give naloxone when opioid exposure is possible, but never let it delay airway support, CPR or the ambulance.

Do not give food, drink, coffee or stimulants, make the person vomit, put them in a shower, or leave them to sleep. Tell responders what may have been taken, what naloxone product was used and when. Preserve packaging or a sample only when safe and without delaying care.

## What this class includes

Opioids include heroin and medicines such as morphine, oxycodone, codeine, tramadol, fentanyl and methadone. They can suppress breathing and consciousness. Tolerance, a prescription, or a familiar-looking product does not prevent overdose.

Nitazenes and illicitly manufactured fentanyls are potent synthetic opioids. They may replace or contaminate heroin or counterfeit tablets, including products sold as opioid painkillers or street benzodiazepines. The UK evidence supports urgent warnings about nitazenes and changing contamination, but it does not show that illicit fentanyl is in every UK product. Follow current official festival and public-health alerts; do not import claims from another country's drug market.

A logo, tablet imprint, packaging, appearance, seller claim or home-test result cannot establish identity or strength. See [Unknown substances, counterfeit products and opioid contamination](wiki:drug-testing.unknown-and-counterfeit) and [Professional and at-home drug checking](wiki:drug-testing.professional-and-at-home).

## Xylazine contamination

Xylazine is a non-opioid veterinary sedative that has been detected in the UK illicit supply, including alongside opioids. It can add prolonged unconsciousness, low blood pressure and breathing depression. Naloxone does not reverse xylazine itself, but should still be given when an opioid may also be present.

Emergency care and breathing support remain essential even after naloxone. Persistent sedation after naloxone does not prove xylazine and is not a reason to stop care. Severe or spreading skin ulcers have been reported among people injecting xylazine-containing drugs and need prompt clinical assessment, but current UK evidence does not establish that xylazine is common at festivals.

## Dangerous combinations

Alcohol, benzodiazepines and Z-drugs, GHB/GBL, pregabalin, gabapentin and other sedatives can combine with opioids to suppress breathing and consciousness. Ketamine adds severe impairment, vomiting and aspiration risk. Stimulants do not protect the airway and can hide drowsiness while poisoning continues. See [Dangerous drug combinations](wiki:drug-testing.dangerous-combinations).

Tramadol also carries seizure and serotonergic risks. Combining it with MDMA, MAOIs, some antidepressants or other serotonergic drugs can cause serious toxicity. Do not stop prescribed medicine abruptly to avoid an interaction; ask a pharmacist or prescriber.

Some codeine products also contain paracetamol, ibuprofen or aspirin. Taking more than directed can therefore cause additional poisoning even when opioid signs are absent. Bring the packet to clinicians when safe.

## Dependence, withdrawal and reduced tolerance

Opioids can cause tolerance, dependence and addiction, including when prescribed. Suddenly stopping a prescribed opioid can cause withdrawal; ask the prescriber or a drug service for a supported reduction plan rather than stopping abruptly or self-treating with an unknown product.

Tolerance can fall after stopping, detoxification, hospital care or time in custody. Returning to an amount previously used can then cause a fatal overdose. A recent non-fatal overdose is a reason for urgent clinical and drug-service follow-up and replacement naloxone.

Withdrawal symptoms can include sweating, agitation, vomiting, diarrhoea, abdominal cramps, muscle aches and sleep disturbance. Severe dehydration, confusion, collapse, seizure, chest pain, pregnancy-related concerns or immediate danger needs urgent medical assessment; emergency signs require [Get emergency help](wiki:emergency.get-help).
