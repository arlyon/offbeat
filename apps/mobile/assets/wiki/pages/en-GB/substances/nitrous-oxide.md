# Nitrous oxide

> **CALL 999 OR 112 NOW** if someone has collapsed, cannot be woken, is not breathing normally, has a seizure, has severe difficulty breathing, has sudden severe chest pain or is rapidly getting worse. Follow [Get emergency help](wiki:emergency.get-help). Do not wait to identify the gas or for its effects to wear off.

Nitrous oxide, also called laughing gas, nitrous or NOS, is a gas with anaesthetic effects. Medical nitrous oxide is supplied with oxygen and clinical monitoring; an unregulated cylinder, canister or balloon is not equivalent. The contents and equipment may also be uncertain.

## Oxygen deprivation and immediate injury

Breathing nitrous oxide can displace oxygen. Loss of consciousness, brain injury and fatal asphyxia can happen, especially in a car, tent or other enclosed or poorly ventilated space, or if a mask or bag prevents fresh air reaching the person. A person who seems asleep may be critically short of oxygen.

Confusion, dizziness, loss of coordination and fainting can cause falls, head injuries, burns, traffic injuries and drowning. Keep an impaired person away from roads, vehicles, water, heights, fires, cooking equipment and crowd pressure. Stay with them and involve festival medical staff early.

If someone is unconscious but breathing normally, put them in the recovery position and keep checking their breathing. If they are not breathing normally, start CPR and follow the 999 or 112 call handler. Do not give food, drink or another drug, make them vomit, or leave them to sleep.

## Cylinders, cold burns and fire

> **Never inhale directly from a pressurised cylinder or tank.** Rapidly released gas and freezing equipment can injure the mouth, throat, lungs, eyes and skin. Cold injury may not be noticed immediately because nitrous oxide reduces pain.

Treat suspected cold burns to the mouth, throat, eyes or skin as needing medical assessment. Breathing difficulty, chest pain or swelling around the airway is an emergency. Do not tamper with, puncture or heat a cylinder. Nitrous oxide is not flammable, but it supports combustion; keep cylinders and canisters away from flames and never put them in a fire.

## Repeated or high-volume use

Repeated or heavy exposure can inactivate vitamin B12 and damage the spinal cord and peripheral nerves. Early warning signs include persistent tingling or numbness in the hands or feet. Weak legs, an unsteady or staggering walk, muscle stiffness, twitching, or new bladder or bowel problems can follow.

Stop exposure and seek urgent medical assessment for any of these neurological signs. Tell clinicians about nitrous oxide even if use was days or weeks earlier. Sudden or worsening weakness, inability to walk safely, collapse or breathing difficulty needs emergency help.

Vitamin B12 supplements do not make continued nitrous oxide use safe. Official review evidence reports neurological injury despite supplementation when exposure continued. Assessment may require more than a routine B12 result, and treatment must be directed by clinicians.

## Combinations and unknown products

Alcohol, sedatives and other drugs can worsen impairment, falls, unconsciousness and the chance that danger signs are missed. No combination removes oxygen-deprivation or pressure risks. Stimulants do not protect the airway or prevent nerve damage. See [Dangerous drug combinations](wiki:drug-testing.dangerous-combinations).

Tell medical staff about all drugs, alcohol and medicines that may be involved. Keep the cylinder or packaging for identification only when it can be left secure and handled without delaying care or exposing anyone to leaking gas, cold surfaces or pressure hazards.
