# Ketamine

Ketamine is a dissociative anaesthetic. Illicit powder or liquid cannot be identified by appearance, taste or effects, and strength or contaminants may be unknown.

## Main risks

Ketamine can cause detachment from surroundings, confusion, reduced pain awareness, poor coordination and immobility. Falls, traffic, heights, fire, cold exposure and water become especially dangerous because a person may not recognise injury or be able to move safely.

Vomiting while heavily impaired or unconscious can block the airway. Reduced consciousness and abnormal breathing are emergencies. Memory loss and confusion can also increase vulnerability to assault and make consent impossible.

Repeated use can inflame and damage the bladder and urinary tract. Warning symptoms include urgency, pain when urinating, abdominal or bladder pain and blood in urine. Heavy or frequent use may also be associated with abdominal pain and dependence.

## Combinations

Alcohol, opioids, GHB/GBL, benzodiazepines and other sedatives increase impairment, unconsciousness, vomiting and aspiration risk. Stimulants do not make severe ketamine impairment safe and can add cardiovascular strain. See [Dangerous drug combinations](wiki:drug-testing.dangerous-combinations).

## Reduce immediate risk

The lowest-risk option is not to use. Do not mix ketamine with alcohol or other depressants. Do not go near roads, vehicles, heights, cooking equipment or water when impaired. A person who is dissociated should not walk unassisted.

Have a sober person stay nearby. Do not give food or drink to someone who is very drowsy or cannot sit and swallow normally. Do not make them vomit or put them in a shower or bath.

## Emergency signs

If someone cannot be woken, is not breathing normally, has a seizure, collapses, has blue or grey skin or lips, is repeatedly vomiting while very drowsy, has severe chest pain or is rapidly worsening, follow [Get emergency help](wiki:emergency.get-help) immediately.

Airway and breathing take priority over identifying the drug or estimating when effects should end. Never leave an unconscious person alone or assume they can sleep it off.

## Longer-term help

Urinary pain, blood in urine, severe abdominal pain or reduced bladder capacity need prompt medical assessment. A GP or drug service can support reduction or stopping. Be honest about ketamine because earlier assessment may limit further harm.
