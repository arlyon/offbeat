# Solvents and volatile inhalants

> **CALL 999 OR 112 NOW** if someone has collapsed, cannot be woken, is not breathing normally, has a seizure, has severe chest pain, has severe difficulty breathing, has blue or grey lips or skin, or is rapidly getting worse. Follow [Get emergency help](wiki:emergency.get-help). Do not wait to identify the chemical or for the effects to wear off.

> **Do not enter a tent, vehicle, container or other space filled with fumes.** Keep yourself and others away, alert stewards, security or the fire service, and tell the emergency call handler about the fumes. Move a person towards fresh air only if this can be done without exposing you or anyone else to the atmosphere, fire, traffic or pressurised containers.

Volatile inhalants are a changing class of gases and chemical vapours from products such as aerosols, butane and other gases, glues, petrol and other fuels, paints and thinners, correcting or cleaning fluids, and solvents. Household or commercial availability does not make them safe to inhale. Different products contain different chemicals, propellants and mixtures.

## Hypoxia, asphyxia and loss of consciousness

Vapours and gases can displace oxygen, interfere with breathing and depress the brain. Enclosed or poorly ventilated spaces greatly increase the danger. Anything covering the nose and mouth or preventing access to fresh air can cause fatal asphyxia.

Dizziness, confusion, slurred speech, loss of coordination, hallucinations, vomiting, blackouts and breathing difficulty can appear quickly. Falls, road injuries, drowning and other accidents can follow. Keep an impaired person away from traffic, water, heights, machinery, fire and crowd pressure.

If they are unconscious but breathing normally, put them in the recovery position and keep checking their breathing. If they are not breathing normally, start CPR and follow the 999 or 112 call handler. Do not give food, drink, coffee or another drug, make them vomit, put them in a shower, or leave them to sleep.

## Sudden cardiac death

Some volatile chemicals can trigger a fatal abnormal heart rhythm and sudden cardiac death, including in someone with no known heart problem. Collapse may occur with little warning. Severe chest pain, collapse, an irregular or very fast heartbeat, seizure or sudden breathing difficulty is an emergency even if exposure seemed brief or the person initially appeared well.

Do not make an affected person run or walk around, frighten them, or give a stimulant in an attempt to wake them. Keep the situation calm while emergency help is called.

## Vomiting and aspiration

Confusion or unconsciousness makes choking on vomit and aspiration into the lungs more likely. Put an unconscious person who is breathing normally in the recovery position, remain with them and continue to check breathing. Abnormal breathing, repeated vomiting with reduced consciousness, blue or grey colour, choking sounds or inability to wake requires immediate emergency help.

## Pressurised products and cold burns

Rapidly released gas or aerosol propellant can freeze and injure the lips, mouth, throat, lungs, eyes and skin. Airway swelling or lung injury can become life-threatening. Breathing difficulty, chest pain, voice change, severe coughing, or swelling or cold injury around the mouth or throat requires urgent medical assessment; abnormal breathing is an emergency.

Do not rub a cold burn, apply ice or creams, or tamper with a pressurised canister. Tell medical staff which product was involved and whether it contacted the mouth, eyes, skin or airway.

## Fire, explosion and enclosed-space risks

Many glues, fuels, solvents, gases and aerosols are highly flammable. Vapour can travel and ignite at a flame, cigarette, hot surface, spark or electrical source, causing burns, explosion or a wider fire. Keep people away from the affected area and ignition sources. Do not puncture, heat or burn a container, investigate a leak yourself, or re-enter an enclosed space to retrieve a product.

For a fire, leaking cylinder or strong fumes, move away, warn others and alert festival staff or emergency services. Burns, eye exposure, persistent pain, blistering or a large affected area need medical assessment. Severe burns, facial or airway burns, breathing difficulty or collapse requires [Get emergency help](wiki:emergency.get-help).

## Repeated exposure, combinations and support

Repeated exposure can lead to tolerance and psychological dependence. Withdrawal symptoms including anxiety, irritability and headaches have been reported. Long-term use can damage the brain and movement control, muscles, liver and kidneys. A GP or local drug service can support stopping and assess persistent symptoms.

Alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, GHB/GBL and other depressants can add confusion, vomiting, unconsciousness, aspiration and breathing risks. Stimulants do not prevent hypoxia or cardiac arrest. See [Dangerous drug combinations](wiki:drug-testing.dangerous-combinations).

[Nitrous oxide](wiki:substances.nitrous-oxide) and [poppers](wiki:substances.poppers-alkyl-nitrites) have distinct risks covered on their own pages. If the gas or liquid is not clearly identified, treat it as an [unknown substance](wiki:drug-testing.unknown-and-counterfeit) and act on symptoms.

There is no imported reference record for volatile inhalants. OFFBEAT provides no breath count, container method, inhalation technique or class-wide dose: products and concentrations differ, oxygen availability changes rapidly, and any exposure can be fatal.
