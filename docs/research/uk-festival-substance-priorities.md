# UK festival substance priorities for an offline harm-prevention guide

**Research date:** 2026-08-11
**Decision scope:** Which additional UK-facing substance profiles or warnings merit scarce offline space. Psilocybin mushrooms and cannabis are already included. This is an editorial prioritisation report, not medical advice, a prevalence forecast for any particular festival, or a substitute for current event alerts.

## Executive recommendation

The smallest defensible first expansion is **five new pages plus shared contamination and interaction content**:

1. **Nitrous oxide — standalone page (highest festival-specific gap).** It has direct official evidence of festival use, remains more common among young adults than most candidates despite a recent fall, and has distinctive hypoxia/cold-burn and vitamin-B12-related neurological harms.[S1][S10]
2. **Benzodiazepines and street benzos — class page.** They are not shown to be common across festivals, but the national harm burden, counterfeit/substitution problem and interaction risk are too large to omit. The page should cover prescribed benzodiazepines, street tablets, bromazolam and Z-drugs, with a prominent nitazene/counterfeit warning.[S2][S5][S7]
3. **Opioids, heroin and potent synthetic opioids — class page.** Festival prevalence is low, but opioids dominate UK drug-death and treatment indicators. Put heroin, fentanyl, nitazenes, codeine and tramadol on one page, with naloxone and overdose recognition prominent; do not make separate fentanyl or nitazene “drug experience” pages.[S2][S4][S5][S8]
4. **Other stimulants: speed, cathinones and methamphetamine — class page.** Lead with amphetamine/speed; give mephedrone/4-MMC, 3-MMC/2-MMC and methamphetamine clearly separated subsections. This is more proportionate than three standalone pages: amphetamine is nightlife-relevant, while current UK general-population estimates for mephedrone and methamphetamine are very low and European cathinone signals cannot be treated as UK prevalence.[S1][S15]
5. **Poppers/alkyl nitrites — standalone page.** The strongest direct UK festival evidence among the requested additions after nitrous oxide comes from the ACMD review. The content is short but distinct: never swallow, flammability/chemical injury, fainting and methaemoglobinaemia, visual injury, and the potentially severe blood-pressure interaction with sildenafil/tadalafil/vardenafil.[S11]

In parallel, add three reusable safety blocks rather than more profiles:

- **Unknown/counterfeit contamination:** nitazenes in heroin and counterfeit tablets/street benzos; synthetic cannabinoids in purported THC/CBD/cannabis vapes; variable products sold as “tusi/pink cocaine”; and xylazine in the opioid market.[S7][S8][S9]
- **Respiratory-depressant combinations:** opioids + benzodiazepines/Z-drugs + gabapentinoids + alcohol/GHB/GBL/ketamine. The evidence shows that UK deaths commonly involve multiple substances, not isolated single-drug exposure.[S2][S5][S6]
- **Special interactions:** poppers + PDE5 inhibitors; tramadol + serotonergic drugs and its seizure risk; MAOI-containing preparations + serotonergic/stimulant drugs; and the warning not to abruptly stop prescribed benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids or Z-drugs.[S11][S13][S16]

**Do not initially add standalone pages** for DMT, methamphetamine, pregabalin/gabapentin, codeine, tramadol, fentanyl, nitazenes or “tusi”. Give them the class, warning or interaction placement above. A **synthetic-cannabinoid class page is the first reserve addition** if space permits; for the minimum release, a substantial contamination/emergency section on the existing cannabis page and the unknown/counterfeit page is sufficient.

## 1. How the priority categories are used

The requested categories need one qualification: “common” can mean common in the general population, common in a specific nightlife subgroup, or common in deaths/treatment. Those are not interchangeable. This report therefore uses:

- **A — festival/nightlife-relevant:** supported by a UK festival study reviewed by an official body, an official festival-specific assessment, or a strong nightlife association. It does not mean most festival attendees use it.
- **B — nationally important but less festival-specific:** prominent in UK mortality, treatment or other harm systems, even where general-population use is low.
- **C — uncommon or unquantified but critical:** merits warning space because a small exposure can cause overdose, because the substance appears unexpectedly, or because a specific combination is high risk.
- **D — niche/insufficient evidence for a first-release profile:** neither the available UK prevalence nor harm evidence justifies force-fitting it into A, B or C. It may still need a search alias or subsection.

Page priority is based on **actionable difference**, not only prevalence: whether the page changes emergency recognition, first aid, contamination interpretation, a dangerous interaction, or a common misconception.

## 2. Current UK evidence picture

### 2.1 England and Wales general population and nightlife

The latest Crime Survey for England and Wales (CSEW), year ending March 2025, estimated past-year drug use in 15.1% of people aged 16–24 and 8.7% of people aged 16–59.[S1] Relevant past-year estimates were:

| Substance or survey category | Age 16–59 | Age 16–24 | Interpretation for this guide |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Nitrous oxide | 0.45% | 1.34% | Down significantly from 0.94% and 3.29% respectively in YE March 2024, but still one of the better-supported festival additions. |
| Amphetamines (excluding methamphetamine) | 0.24% | 0.23% | Low nationally, but nightlife association and WEDINOS presence make speed relevant. |
| Mephedrone | 0.064% | 0.057% | Very low estimate; too imprecise and narrow to represent all current cathinones. |
| Methamphetamine | 0.023% | 0.024% | Very low national estimate; no basis for calling it common at UK festivals. |
| Tranquillisers | 0.33% | 0.39% | This self-report category is not equivalent to all illicit or prescribed benzodiazepine use. Mortality and treatment data better show harm burden. |
| Heroin | 0.052% | survey estimate recorded as 0 | A zero survey estimate is not proof of no use; CSEW misses important high-risk populations. |
| New psychoactive substances (generic) | 0.26% | 0.57% | Too broad to estimate synthetic-cannabinoid or cathinone prevalence separately. |
| Ketamine (already included) | 0.80% | 2.02% | Useful benchmark: substantially higher than most requested additions. |
| Ecstasy/MDMA (already included) | 1.18% | 1.94% | Useful benchmark for festival/nightlife prioritisation. |
| Powder cocaine (already included) | 2.07% | 2.3% | Useful benchmark and remains the second-most-used drug after cannabis. |

These values come from ONS appendix tables 1.02 and 1.08, not rounded prose alone.[S1a]

The same dataset supports a nightlife association but not festival causation. Among people aged 16–59 who had visited a nightclub four or more times in the previous month, 32.0% reported any past-year drug use, 25.6% cannabis, 16.0% any Class A drug, 12.6% powder cocaine, 6.1% ecstasy and 2.1% amphetamine. Among those reporting no nightclub visits, the corresponding estimates were 7.4%, 5.5%, 2.5%, 1.5%, 0.8% and 0.19%.[S1a] The frequent-nightclub subgroup had an unweighted base of only 113, and these are associations confounded by age and other characteristics; they must not be presented as “drugs taken in nightclubs”.

### 2.2 Death and treatment burden is concentrated in depressants and polysubstance use

In England and Wales, 5,565 drug-poisoning deaths were registered in 2024. Opiates/opioids were involved in 2,621 (47.1%); heroin/morphine in 1,415; any benzodiazepine in 629; pregabalin in 617; gabapentin in 160; codeine not from a compound formulation in 246; tramadol in 213; amphetamine in 111; and fentanyl in 60. Nitazenes were involved in 195 deaths, nearly four times the 52 registrations in 2023.[S2][S2a] A substance “involved” or “mentioned” is not necessarily the only cause.

England's 2024–25 treatment statistics point in the same direction but describe people in specialist treatment, not prevalence. Opiates remained the largest treatment group (138,255 people; 42% of adults in treatment). Among new entrants, 4,161 reported benzodiazepine problems; methamphetamine treatment entrants rose to 968 but remained a small group.[S4]

Scotland's 2024 drug-misuse deaths show an especially strong polysubstance pattern: 1,017 deaths in total; more than one drug implicated in 80%; opiates/opioids in 810 (80%), benzodiazepines in 574 (56%), cocaine in 479 (47%), and gabapentin and/or pregabalin in 374 (37%). Street benzodiazepines were implicated in 474 deaths, including bromazolam in 346. Nitazenes were implicated in 76 deaths, fentanyls in 12, and xylazine in 20; NRS cautions that nitazene and bromazolam testing changed and routine xylazine testing began in 2024.[S5]

Public Health Wales reports 417 drug-poisoning deaths registered in Wales in 2024, including 288 drug-misuse deaths. Opioids appeared in 200 (69.4%) drug-misuse deaths, pregabalin in 58 (20.1%), benzodiazepines in 83 (28.8%; bromazolam in 40), codeine in 20 and tramadol in 9. Multiple substances were recorded in 179 (62.2%).[S6] Welsh hospital, mortality and treatment patterns describe burden in Wales and cannot be generalised to festival attendees.[S6a]

### 2.3 WEDINOS: valuable market intelligence, not prevalence

WEDINOS analysed 7,064 submitted samples in April 2023–March 2024, including community, night-time-economy and criminal-justice samples. Cocaine was the most commonly identified substance overall and in night-time-economy samples; benzodiazepines were the most commonly identified class; amphetamine was in the community post-analysis top ten.[S7]

The report documents high-consequence substitutions:

- 42% of community submissions with a stated purchase intent did not contain only what was expected;
- bromazolam was listed as purchase intent 13 times but identified 715 times;
- among 1,408 samples submitted as diazepam, 75 contained metonitazene, usually with bromazolam;
- among 138 samples containing a nitazene, the stated intended purchases were most often diazepam (75), heroin (24) and oxycodone (22);
- 86 of 211 submitted purported THC/CBD/cannabis vape samples contained one or more synthetic cannabinoid receptor agonists (SCRAs).[S7]

These are reasons for **contamination warnings**, not estimates such as “5% of UK diazepam contains nitazenes” or “41% of UK cannabis vapes contain spice”. WEDINOS explicitly says its service is self-selecting and its nitazene data are not an indicator of prevalence. Submissions were affected by active incident investigations and targeted encouragement; night-time-economy samples were amnesty-bin items or unattributed finds and had no purchase-intent or demographic data.[S7]

## 4. Better-supported omissions

### 4.1 Xylazine — contamination warning, not standalone page

WEDINOS reported xylazine in a submitted heroin sample and in injected samples. Scotland recorded 20 xylazine-implicated deaths in 2024, while warning that routine testing began only that year. Government preparedness guidance describes it as increasingly seen in the heroin market.[S5][S7][S9] The updated ACMD review also records two seized blotters from one south-of-England festival in 2023 that contained xylazine with fluoroexetamine; this establishes possible unexpected festival exposure, not prevalence.[S14] Add xylazine to the opioid contamination block. Naloxone is still indicated when opioid exposure is possible, but it is not an antidote to xylazine itself; emergency care and breathing support remain essential.[S14] Current evidence does not justify portraying xylazine as common at festivals.

### 4.2 Z-drugs and bromazolam — include with benzodiazepines

A page titled only “diazepam/Xanax” would miss the strongest UK market signal: street products often contain a different benzodiazepine, particularly bromazolam, and WEDINOS also found nitazenes in purported diazepam.[S5][S7] Include zopiclone and zolpidem as related sedatives because the same dependence/withdrawal and combination warnings are actionable, while making clear they are not benzodiazepines chemically.[S13]

### 4.3 Semi-synthetic cannabinoids and cannabis-format products

EUDA 2026 describes rapid diversification of semi-synthetic cannabinoids, variable vape/edible products and poisoning risk, but these are European signals rather than UK prevalence.[S15a] For a UK minimum guide, update the existing cannabis/unknown-product warning to cover purported THC/CBD/HHC-style vapes and edibles rather than adding separate HHC pages. Keep this under surveillance.

### 4.4 Counterfeit tablets is a content type, not a substance

The recurring UK signal crosses classes: purported diazepam, alprazolam, oxycodone and other tablets may contain bromazolam, nitazenes or different ingredients.[S7][S8] A reusable “counterfeit tablet” card with search aliases will prevent duplicating the same warning across profiles and is more durable as substances change.

## 5. Recommended information architecture

### Priority 0: required before calling the offline set UK-ready

1. **Nitrous oxide** — standalone.
2. **Benzodiazepines and street benzos** — class page.
3. **Opioids, heroin and potent synthetic opioids** — class page.
4. **Universal contamination/counterfeit card** — cross-linked from cannabis, cocaine, MDMA, ketamine, benzodiazepines and opioids.
5. **Expanded dangerous-combinations card** — depressant stacking, tramadol/serotonergic drugs, poppers/PDE5 inhibitors, MAOIs.
6. **Naloxone/opioid-overdose emergency block** — available offline and duplicated where an emergency user can reach it quickly.

### Priority 1: smallest festival-relevance expansion

1. **Other stimulants: speed, cathinones and methamphetamine** — one class page with distinct subsections.
2. **Poppers/alkyl nitrites** — standalone short page.
3. **Synthetic cannabinoids** — class page if space permits; otherwise keep the substantial contamination/emergency coverage in cannabis and unknown products.

### Priority 2: only after usage analytics or new UK alerts justify them

- standalone gabapentinoid page;
- standalone DMT page;
- separate methamphetamine page;
- separate cathinone page;
- separate codeine or tramadol pages.

Never make “tusi” a fixed-composition profile. Keep it as an alias to unknown/counterfeit mixtures.

## 6. Minimum content requirements by format

| Format | Must contain |
|---|---|
| Standalone/class substance page | UK names/aliases; uncertainty of identity/strength; major acute and delayed harms; combinations; dependence/withdrawal where relevant; emergency signs; what festival medical staff need to know; current source date. |
| Contamination warning | Expected product; unexpected high-consequence classes; why appearance/branding/home tests cannot exclude them; current alert route; naloxone relevance; “symptoms override test results”. |
| Interaction subsection | Both classes and common names; mechanism stated conservatively; practical consequence; emergency signs; explicit instruction not to stop prescribed medicine without clinical advice. |
| Search alias/redirect | Street name leads to the safety content without implying that the label identifies composition. This is essential for “tusi”, “Xanax”, “Valium”, “oxy”, “spice”, “THC vape”, “tina”, “4-MMC” and “laughing gas”. |

## 8. Geographic and data limitations

1. **No single “UK festival prevalence” dataset exists.** CSEW is England and Wales household self-report; NRS covers Scottish registered deaths; Public Health Wales covers Wales; England treatment data do not cover the other nations. Northern Ireland is underrepresented in this review.
2. **CSEW excludes important populations and has unstable rare-drug estimates.** It covers private households and does not represent prisons, many people experiencing homelessness, or other communal settings. Very small percentages and zeros should not be treated as exact market shares or absence.[S1]
3. **Nightclub association is not place of use.** ONS asks about past-year drug use and past-month venue attendance separately. The small frequent-nightclub subgroup and confounding prevent causal or venue-specific claims.[S1a]
4. **Festival surveys are convenience samples.** The English Festival Survey evidence cited by ACMD is direct and useful, but respondents and festivals were not a representative sample of all UK festivals. The 8.3% poppers figure must retain its sample, years and two-festival limitation.[S11]
5. **Deaths are registrations and commonly polysubstance.** England/Wales 2024 registrations include deaths that may have occurred earlier; median registration delay was 214 days in England and 279 in Wales. A mentioned drug is not necessarily the sole cause. National definitions and toxicology practices differ.[S2][S5][S6]
6. **Testing changes trends.** NRS notes limited earlier testing for nitazenes and bromazolam and routine xylazine testing beginning in 2024. Apparent increases can partly reflect detection.[S5]
7. **Treatment and hospital data measure service contact, not use prevalence.** They overrepresent people with more severe or recognised problems and reflect access and coding practices.[S4][S6a]
8. **WEDINOS is deliberately self-selecting.** It receives concern-driven, incident-driven, service, amnesty-bin and individual submissions. Its aggregated findings show what can occur and identify substitutions; they cannot estimate the proportion of the retail market or festival attendees using a drug.[S7]
9. **EUDA is context, not UK measurement.** The UK is not an EU Member State in the 2026 EU aggregates. EUDA's cathinone, methamphetamine, cannabinoid and opioid trends justify surveillance and flexible class content, not claims about UK prevalence.[S15][S15a]
10. **Public alerts are time-sensitive.** An offline guide must display its content date and direct users to the festival's official medical/welfare and drug-alert channels whenever connectivity exists. Do not encode an old alert as a permanent market fact.[S9]
