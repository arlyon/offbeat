# Source review: short festival mobility and stretching guidance

**Research date:** 2026-08-11

**Scope:** Brief, field-suitable lower-limb mobility and stretching for generally healthy adults at festivals, focused on hips, knees, ankles, calves, hamstrings, quadriceps and gluteal muscles. This is an evidence-and-content brief, not an individual exercise prescription or a substitute for clinical assessment.

## Executive conclusions

1. Frame a pre-activity sequence as a **gradual warm-up for readiness and comfort**, not as a guarantee against injury. NHS provides a general warm-up routine with marching, heel digs, knee lifts and shallow knee bends, but the evidence for static stretching as an injury-prevention intervention does not support a broad prevention claim.[1][11][12]
2. Frame post-activity stretches as an **optional gentle cool-down/flexibility routine**. Do not say they treat an injury, “flush toxins”, or reliably prevent next-day soreness: a Cochrane review found no clinically important reduction in delayed-onset muscle soreness from stretching before or after exercise.[2][10]
3. Use only slow, controlled, pain-free movements. A Worcestershire Acute Hospitals NHS Trust physiotherapy leaflet says stretches should be gradual, slow and smooth, with a sustained hold and without excessive force.[6] NHS exercise safety wording is stricter and simpler for a public guide: **stop immediately if there is pain or the person becomes unwell**.[1]
4. Keep published doses attached to the exact source exercise and context. General NHS cool-down stretches are held for **15–20 seconds**; its seated ankle mobility uses **2 sets of 5 per foot**. Do not manufacture a universal dose or combine timings from clinically targeted leaflets as if they were interchangeable.[2][4][6]
5. Do not use a mobility card as treatment for an acute sprain, strain, suspected fracture, hot/swollen joint, or unexplained one-sided calf swelling. Provide clear stop, NHS 111, A&E/999 and DVT escalation routes.[7][8][9][13]

## Evidence framing and wording guardrails

### Safe claims

- “A gradual warm-up can help you get ready to move.” This is a conservative rendering of the NHS warm-up page, which supplies a progressive routine and describes warming up before more rigorous activity.[1]
- “These optional gentle stretches can be used after activity as a cool-down and for flexibility.” NHS describes its post-workout routine as a way to cool down, gradually relax, improve flexibility and slow heart rate.[2]
- “Stretch only to a comfortable position; move slowly and smoothly; do not force it.” This follows NHS Trust physiotherapy form guidance.[6]
- “Stop immediately if it hurts or you feel unwell.” This is explicit NHS safety advice.[1]

### Claims to avoid or qualify

- **Do not promise injury prevention.** The NHS warm-up page itself uses injury-prevention language,[1] but research specifically on static stretching does not justify turning a short stretch card into an injury-prevention claim. A UK-authored systematic review found moderate-to-strong evidence that routine static stretching did not reduce overall injury rates, although it noted preliminary evidence for some musculotendinous injuries.[11] An earlier BMJ systematic review found no practically useful reduction in injury risk in the two included military-recruit studies and cautioned that generalisability needed testing.[12] The defensible product wording is therefore “warm up gradually”, not “do these stretches to prevent injury”.
- **Do not promise prevention or treatment of delayed-onset muscle soreness (DOMS).** Cochrane’s 12-study review found little or no effect on soreness and no clinically important reduction in healthy adults.[10]
- **Do not present stretching as treatment for current pain or injury.** NHS says its general exercise videos are not aimed at treating a specific cause of pain or condition and directs people with injury, symptoms or recent health events to seek professional advice before trying them.[1]
- **Do not imply one mandatory stretching dose.** NHS general cool-down guidance uses 15–20-second holds,[2] while a hospital physiotherapy leaflet uses at least 30 seconds repeated three times in its own clinical context.[6] Kent Community Health NHS Foundation Trust leaves duration and repetitions blank for a clinician to individualise and says its leaflet should only be followed on healthcare-professional advice.[5] These are context-dependent protocols, not evidence of one universal field dose.

## Source-backed short routines

These are content-building blocks from the cited sources, not a newly validated festival protocol. A final user-facing card should say to choose a clear, stable space and use a secure support where needed. Those environmental checks are sensible festival adaptations, but they are not tested claims from the sources below.

### Before prolonged walking or dancing: gradual lower-limb warm-up

The most field-compatible general NHS sequence is:[1]

| Movement | Source-backed form | Published amount | Main areas involved |
|---|---|---:|---|
| March on the spot, then forwards/backwards if space permits | Pump bent arms in rhythm; keep fists soft | 3 minutes | Hips, knees, ankles; general temperature/readiness |
| Alternate heel digs | Place one heel forward with toes pointing up; retain a slight bend in the supporting leg | 60 digs in 60 seconds | Ankles, calves, knees |
| Alternate knee lifts | Stand tall; bring each knee towards the opposite hand; keep back straight and supporting knee slightly bent | 30 lifts in 30 seconds | Hips, glutes, knees |
| Shallow knee bends | Feet shoulder-width apart; lower no more than 10 cm by bending the knees, then rise | 10 repetitions | Knees, quadriceps, glutes |

The full NHS warm-up also includes shoulder rolls and is described as taking **at least 6 minutes**.[1] A lower-limb-only excerpt should not be labelled the complete NHS six-minute routine. If a person is going directly into vigorous dancing, the source supports warming up longer if needed, but it does not define a personalised intensity test.[1]

**Festival-safe editorial adaptation:** in a crowd, substitute easy walking or marching in place for travelling movements. Avoid deep squats, ballistic leg swings and forced end-range stretches; none is needed to reproduce the cited general NHS sequence.

### After activity: optional gentle lower-limb stretches

NHS presents the complete routine as a roughly five-minute cool-down, with more time if needed.[2] The target-area exercises are:[2]

| Stretch | Source-backed form | Published amount | Target |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Buttock/gluteal stretch | Lie on the back, knees towards chest; cross one leg over the opposite thigh; hold behind the uncrossed thigh and draw it towards the chest; repeat other side | 15–20 seconds each side | Gluteal/buttock and hip area |
| Hamstring stretch | Lie on the back; raise one leg; hold below the knee; keep the other leg bent with foot down; draw the raised, straight leg towards the body; repeat | 15–20 seconds each side | Hamstrings |
| Inner-thigh stretch | Sit upright with knees bent and soles together; hold the feet and gently lower knees towards the ground | 15–20 seconds | Hip adductors/inner thighs |
| Calf stretch | Step one leg forward and bend it; lean slightly forward while keeping the rear leg straight and trying to lower its heel; use a wall or chair for support if needed; repeat | 15–20 seconds each side | Calf and ankle dorsiflexion position |
| Front-thigh stretch | Lie on one side; hold the top foot and gently draw heel towards buttock; keep knees touching; repeat | 15–20 seconds each side | Quadriceps/front thigh |

The ground-based positions may be impractical on wet, uneven or crowded festival terrain. **Omitting a stretch is safer than improvising an unstable version.** Where secure support or a stable chair exists, the source-backed alternatives below are available.

## Supported and seated alternatives

### Secure wall/chair support

- **Supported standing calf stretch:** NHS permits leaning on a wall or chair for its 15–20-second calf stretch.[2] Its flexibility page also describes placing hands on a wall, keeping both feet flat, the rear knee straight and rear heel down, and performing three on each side; that page does not specify a hold duration for this movement.[3]
- **Supported standing quadriceps form:** Kent Community Health NHS Foundation Trust describes standing next to a wall or other support, bending the knee, holding the ankle, drawing the foot towards the buttock, and keeping the back straight and knees together.[5] The Trust deliberately leaves hold/repetition fields for a clinician and says the leaflet is to be followed only on professional advice, so a general guide should not borrow an unsourced dose.
- **Standing hamstring form:** the same Kent leaflet describes placing a foot on an object below waist height, keeping legs straight, hips forward and back straight; use a lower object if needed and hinge forward with a straight back only to increase the stretch.[5] Because this is clinician-directed material and requires a genuinely stable object, the NHS general lying stretch is the safer default; otherwise omit it.

### Stable-chair options

The NHS sitting routine specifies a **solid, stable chair without wheels**, feet flat and knees at right angles.[4]

- **Seated hip march:** sit upright without leaning on the chair back, hold the chair sides, lift one bent knee only as far as comfortable, lower with control, and alternate; **5 lifts per leg**.[4]
- **Seated ankle point/flex:** sit upright holding the chair, straighten and raise one leg, point toes away and then back; **2 sets of 5 per foot**.[4] Use this as an ankle-mobility alternative, not as treatment for ankle injury and not as a guarantee against blood clots.
- **Seated hamstring alternative:** the NHS runner page describes sitting at the chair edge, keeping one leg bent, placing the other heel down with that leg straight, keeping the back straight, and hinging towards the straight leg from the hips; **3 sets of 15 seconds per leg**.[9] That page says the exercises should not cause pain and are unsuitable for an existing knee injury.[9]

## Form and field-safety principles

### Directly supported by clinical sources

- Build up gradually and keep movements controlled.[1][3][4]
- Stretch gradually with a sustained hold; move slowly and smoothly; do not apply excessive force.[6]
- For standing calf work, keep the rear heel on the floor and use a wall/chair for stability.[2][3][6]
- For the quadriceps position, keep the back straight and knees together; use support for the standing version.[2][5]
- For supported/seated options, use stable equipment; NHS expressly excludes wheeled chairs.[4]
- **Pain is not a target:** NHS says stop immediately if any exercise causes pain or the person becomes unwell.[1][9]

### Festival-specific editorial safeguards

These are prudent adaptations, not separately trialled interventions:

- Move away from dense crowds, slopes, mud, tent lines, broken glass and traffic before balancing or sitting.
- Keep footwear on unless the surface is known to be safe.
- Do not use another unsteady person, a loose barrier, tent pole, folding camping chair or wheeled chair as support.
- Keep breathing normally; do not compete over depth or range.
- If tired, intoxicated, dizzy, overheated or poorly coordinated, stop rather than attempting balance-based movements; seek welfare/medical help if unwell.

## Contraindications, cautions and stop rules

### Get individual advice before using a general routine

NHS advises contacting a healthcare professional first if the person is unsure the exercises suit their fitness; has a health problem, injury, symptoms or feels unwell; has had a recent health event such as a heart attack or operation; has other health concerns; or is pregnant or recently postpartum.[1] The NHS post-exercise page also says injury, a medical condition or pregnancy may require modifications selected with a physiotherapist or other healthcare professional.[2]

A public festival card should therefore not prescribe these movements for:

- an existing or new hip, knee, ankle or leg injury;
- current unexplained pain, marked swelling, redness, heat, numbness or weakness;
- a recently operated or clinician-restricted joint/limb;
- anyone whose clinician has limited weight-bearing or range of motion.

This does not mean movement is always prohibited; it means a generic card cannot determine the appropriate movement.

### Stop now

Stop the movement or stretch immediately if:[1][9]

- it causes pain (rather than a comfortable sense of stretch);
- pain increases with each repetition or hold;
- the person feels unwell.

If symptoms are concerning or do not settle, NHS directs users to NHS 111 online or by phone.[1]

### Acute sprain/strain: do not stretch through it

For the first two to three days after a suspected sprain or strain, NHS advises protection, rest from exercise/activity, ice for up to 20 minutes every two to three hours, compression and elevation; it also advises avoiding heat, alcohol and massage for the first couple of days.[7] Movement is resumed only when the injured area can move **without pain stopping it**.[7] Therefore a festival mobility routine must not be positioned as acute injury treatment or a “walk it off” instruction.

## Red flags and when to seek help

### NHS 111 / urgent treatment advice

After an injury, get NHS 111 help when pain is very bad or worsening; swelling/bruising is large or worsening; the person cannot put weight on it or walk more than a few steps; it is very stiff or difficult to move; it is not improving with self-care; or there is a very high temperature or hot/cold/shivery feeling suggesting possible infection.[7]

Additional region-specific reasons include:

- **Knee:** cannot move or bear weight; badly swollen or changed shape; locks, gives way or painfully clicks; or fever/systemic symptoms with redness or heat around the knee.[8]
- **Ankle:** severe pain, faintness/dizziness/sickness from pain, changed shape/odd angle, a snap/grinding/popping noise at injury, or inability to walk.[13]

At a festival, contact onsite medical/welfare staff for assessment while arranging the appropriate NHS route; do not continue the routine to “test” the limb.

### A&E / 999 red flags

NHS sprain/strain guidance says call 999 or go to A&E if a crack was heard, the body part has changed shape or points at an odd angle, there is numbness/tingling/pins and needles, or nearby skin is blue/grey or cold.[7] The broken-ankle page additionally lists exposed bone, a bad wound, severe pain, and toes that are blue/white or numb as A&E indications.[14]

### One-sided calf pain/swelling may not be muscle tightness

NHS lists throbbing pain in one calf or thigh, one-sided swelling, red/blue/darkened skin around the painful area, and swollen veins as possible DVT symptoms; seek an urgent GP appointment or NHS 111.[15] Call 999 or go to A&E if these occur with shortness of breath or chest pain because pulmonary embolism can be life-threatening.[15] A stretch card should say **do not massage or stretch unexplained one-sided calf pain/swelling as if it were ordinary tightness**.

## Suggested content hierarchy for a festival safety card

1. **Top warning:** “For generally healthy adults. Not treatment for pain or injury. Stop if it hurts or you feel unwell.”[1][9]
2. **Before activity:** gradual march → heel digs → knee lifts → shallow knee bends, using the exact NHS form/dose if space and ability permit.[1]
3. **After activity:** optional gentle calf, hamstring, front-thigh and buttock/hip stretches, with 15–20-second holds only for the exact NHS versions.[2]
4. **Accessible alternative:** stable-chair hip marching and ankle point/flex; explicitly require a non-wheeled stable chair.[4]
5. **Escalation:** concise “111 vs A&E/999” injury red flags and one-sided-calf DVT warning.[7][8][13][14][15]
6. **Evidence footer:** “Stretch for comfort/flexibility, not as proven treatment or guaranteed prevention of injury or next-day soreness.”[10][11][12]

## Limitations

- The NHS exercise pages are broad public guidance, not trials of festival attendees or of activity performed on uneven, crowded ground.
- The hospital physiotherapy leaflets were written for clinical populations and should not be converted wholesale into a generic routine. They are used here mainly for form and for identifying where dosing is intentionally individualised.[5][6]
- Injury-prevention reviews are older and study heterogeneous athletic/military populations. They are adequate to reject an unqualified prevention promise, not to prove that all stretching has no effect on every injury subtype.[11][12]
- The Cochrane conclusion concerns delayed-onset soreness in healthy adults, not acute injury diagnosis or treatment.[10]
- Anyone with persistent, recurrent or function-limiting symptoms needs clinical advice rather than progressively more stretching.
