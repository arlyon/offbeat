# UK festival hearing protection and earplug guidance: source review

**Research date:** 2026-08-11
**Scope:** Evidence and editorial guidance for a public-facing UK festival hearing-protection page. This is not individual medical advice, a product endorsement, a substitute for a clinician, or a claim that hearing damage can always be prevented.

## Executive conclusions

1. **Risk depends on sound level, duration and repeated exposure.** Loud sound can cause temporary muffled hearing or tinnitus, but a single extreme exposure or repeated/prolonged exposure can cause permanent noise-induced hearing loss (NIHL), tinnitus, or both.[S1]
2. **Use several protections together:** wear properly fitted hearing-protection earplugs, move away from loudspeakers/amplifiers, and take regular breaks in a genuinely quiet area. WHO says to rest ears in a quiet space for 10 minutes after every hour in noisy environments; RNID gives a less conservative festival suggestion of around five minutes per hour.[S1][S4]
3. **Do not promise a “safe distance” or “safe number of sets”.** Festival arrays, stages, crowd position, reflections, peaks, exposure earlier in the week, earplug fit and individual susceptibility vary. Distance and breaks reduce exposure; they do not reset accumulated dose or guarantee safety.[S1][S2]
4. **Foam and filtered/music earplugs are both legitimate options when designed and rated for hearing protection.** Disposable foam plugs are cheap and can provide useful attenuation when fitted correctly. Filtered or “musician” plugs are designed to preserve music quality better and may be reusable or custom-made, but the label does not make them inherently safer: adequate attenuation and a reliable seal still matter.[S3][S4][S9]
5. **Fit is critical.** For user-formable foam, use clean hands; roll/compress the plug; pull the ear up and back; insert it into the canal to a comfortable depth; and hold it while it expands. A plug sitting only across the canal is not correctly fitted. Refit in a quiet place if it loosens.[S1][S8][S9]
6. **Reuse only products explicitly sold as reusable.** HSE says disposable earplugs should be used once; reusable plugs should be cleaned regularly, checked for damage/degradation, stored correctly, issued to one person and maintained according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Do not share earplugs.[S6][S8][S9]
7. **Package attenuation is not a personal guarantee.** SNR and frequency-band data come from standardised laboratory testing. Real-world protection is usually lower because of fit and use; HSE’s workplace calculation adds a 4 dB real-world allowance after a standardised attenuation calculation. An attendee should not simply subtract an SNR from a phone’s dB(A) reading.[S3][S6][S7][S9]
8. **Occupational values are not attendee guarantees.** HSE’s 80/85 dB(A) action values and 87 dB(A) exposure limit are legal workplace constructs based on daily or weekly employee exposure. HSE expressly says the regulations do not apply to members of the public choosing noisy non-work activities. WHO’s venue limit of 100 dB LAeq,15min is also an upper venue-management limit, measured at a reference position—not a promise that every audience member is safe.[S2][S5][S9]
9. **Sudden hearing loss is time-critical.** NHS says sudden loss in one or both ears needs an urgent GP appointment or NHS 111. NICE says unexplained hearing loss developing over three days or less within the past 30 days should be referred immediately and seen within 24 hours by ENT or an emergency department.[S10][S14]
10. **Use the emergency route for tinnitus with major red flags.** NHS says go to A&E or call 999 for tinnitus after a head injury, or tinnitus with sudden hearing loss, facial weakness or vertigo. Regular/constant, worsening or bothersome tinnitus warrants a GP appointment; pulsatile tinnitus warrants an urgent GP appointment.[S11]

## 1. Noise exposure: what a festival page can safely say

### Supported explanation

WHO describes sound exposure as a combination of **volume, duration and frequency of exposure**. The higher the level and the longer or more frequent the exposure, the greater the risk.[S1] Loud concerts can cause temporary sensory-cell fatigue, heard as ringing/buzzing or muffled hearing. Symptoms may improve, but regular loud or prolonged exposure can permanently damage sensory cells and other structures. NIHL may also occur immediately after an extremely loud burst and is irreversible.[S1]

WHO gives these illustrative weekly exposure equivalents:[S1]

| Average sound level | WHO safe-listening time per 7 days |
|---:|---:|
| 80 dB | 40 hours |
| 85 dB | 12 hours 30 minutes |
| 90 dB | 4 hours |
| 95 dB | 1 hour 15 minutes |
| 100 dB | 20 minutes |
| 105 dB | 8 minutes |
| 110 dB | 2.5 minutes |

These figures illustrate how rapidly permissible duration falls as level rises. They are **not a festival timetable, a diagnostic threshold, or a personal guarantee**. They concern cumulative exposure over seven days, not just one stage or one evening, and do not capture every peak, individual vulnerability or uncertainty in an attendee’s phone measurement.[S1][S2]

A useful no-meter rule from WHO is: if a person must raise their voice to be heard by someone an arm’s length away, the sound is too high.[S1] This is a prompt to add protection or leave for a quieter area, not a calibrated measurement.

### Distance and breaks

Directly supported advice:

- Stay away from loudspeakers and amplifiers; sound intensity reduces as distance from the source increases.[S1]
- RNID’s Audiology Manager says the closer a person is to speakers, the greater the risk, and that even short bursts can damage hearing.[S4]
- WHO recommends resting ears in a **quiet space for 10 minutes after every hour** in a noisy environment.[S1]
- RNID’s festival guidance suggests regular breaks of around **five minutes per hour** from the loudest areas.[S4]

**Recommended editorial choice:** use WHO’s more conservative “10 quiet minutes each hour” while making clear that more quiet time is sensible after louder or longer exposure. “Quiet” should mean away from amplified stages and other loud sources, not merely at the back of another noisy crowd.

Do not publish a universal distance in metres. Outdoor line arrays are designed to project over distance, different loudspeakers may be positioned around an arena, and level varies by venue and measurement position. The defensible wording is **“move well away from speakers; if you still need to shout at arm’s length, keep moving to a quieter area.”**[S1][S2]

Breaks reduce total exposure but do not make earlier exposure disappear. Keep hearing protection in place until actually in a quieter environment; repeated removal for conversation in the loud area substantially reduces overall protection.[S6][S8]

## 2. Occupational limits versus attendee protection

### HSE workplace values

Under the Control of Noise at Work Regulations 2005, HSE lists:[S5][S9]

| Workplace value | Daily/weekly personal exposure | Peak sound pressure |
|---|---:|---:|
| Lower exposure action value | 80 dB(A) | 135 dB(C) |
| Upper exposure action value | 85 dB(A) | 137 dB(C) |
| Exposure limit value | 87 dB(A) | 140 dB(C) |

These values trigger or constrain **employer actions**. The action values are assessed without credit for hearing protection; the 87 dB(A)/140 dB(C) exposure limit values account for properly used hearing protection.[S9] HSE warns that the exposure limit must not be treated as a target for protector performance: risks must be eliminated or reduced as low as reasonably practicable.[S9]

HSE expressly excludes “members of the public exposed to noise from their non-work activities, or making an informed choice to go to noisy places” from these regulations.[S5] Festival employees and contractors may be covered occupationally; ticket holders are not thereby guaranteed an exposure below a workplace value.

### WHO venue/event standard

WHO’s global standard recommends six venue controls: sound below **100 dB LAeq,15min**, live monitoring with calibrated equipment, suitable acoustics/sound systems, audience hearing protection with instructions, quiet zones, and training/information.[S2]

Important qualifications from WHO:[S2]

- 100 dB LAeq,15min is an **upper limit, not a target**; many events should be substantially quieter.
- It is a moving 15-minute A-weighted average at a defined reference measurement position, not the level at every audience member’s ears.
- A lower limit should be used for child-focused events.
- The limit cannot eliminate all risk, especially for frequent attendees.
- WHO’s derivation assumes patterns of frequency and duration; an individual’s other leisure-noise exposure still matters.

**Safe page wording:** “Venue limits and workplace limits help manage population or employee risk. They do not certify that a festival set, location or earplug choice is safe for you.”

## 3. Foam versus filtered/musician earplugs

Use category descriptions, not brands.

| Type | Advantages | Limitations and cautions |
|---|---|---|
| **Disposable user-formable foam** | Inexpensive, widely available and potentially substantial attenuation when fully inserted and sealed. WHO and RNID both describe roll-and-expand fitting.[S1][S3] | Technique-sensitive; can be hard to fit and can loosen. Disposable versions are one-use only. They may alter music quality more than purpose-designed music filters. A partly inserted foam plug may give much less protection than its package rating.[S3][S9] |
| **Pre-moulded reusable plugs** | Quick to insert; washable/reusable when the manufacturer says so; hardwearing.[S3] | May not fit every canal snugly and may attenuate less effectively than a well-fitted custom plug. Must be cleaned, inspected and stored according to instructions.[S3][S9] |
| **Filtered/music or “musician” plugs** | Acoustic filters are intended to preserve tonal balance and speech/music clarity better while reducing level. Available as reusable universal-fit and custom-fit products.[S3][S4] | “Music”, “filtered”, “flat” or “high fidelity” is not proof of adequate attenuation. Compare tested SNR and frequency-band data, ensure the fit is secure, and keep them in for the whole loud exposure. Do not repeat RNID’s phrase “normal but safe levels” as a guarantee.[S3][S7] |
| **Custom-moulded filtered or solid plugs** | Can improve comfort and fit for regular users and may last years.[S3] | More expensive; HSE says poor manufacturing or initial fitting can cause poor performance. Custom does not mean infallible; fit should be confirmed and the product maintained.[S9] |

Additional selection rules:

- Confirm the product is **designed and rated for hearing protection**. RNID warns that products intended for swimming or simply reducing irritating background noise may not protect against damaging noise.[S3]
- RNID recommends an SNR of at least 20 dB for general noise exposure, but this is a broad selection rule, not a guarantee for every festival position or filtered design.[S3]
- Do not say filtered plugs are always more protective than foam. Their main advantage is sound quality and potentially better consistent wear; actual attenuation depends on the exact product and fit.[S3][S4]
- Do not use cotton wool as hearing protection.[S3]
- Comfort matters because a plug that is removed does not protect. It should nevertheless form a seal, not merely rest at the ear opening.[S8][S9]
- Avoid “blocks all harmful noise”, “concert safe”, “hearing-damage proof” or similar absolute claims.

## 4. Correct fitting principles

### User-formable foam plugs

A concise source-backed sequence is:[S1][S3][S8]

1. Move to a quieter, clean place if possible and clean/dry your hands.
2. Roll the plug between thumb and forefinger to compress it into a narrow shape. Follow that product’s instructions rather than inventing a universal rolling time.
3. Reach over with the opposite hand and pull the outer ear **up and back** to open the canal.
4. Insert the compressed plug into the ear canal to a **comfortable depth**. It must go into the canal, not sit only across the opening.
5. Hold it in place while it expands and becomes secure.
6. Fit both ears. If a plug loosens, move to a quiet area and refit or replace it.[S9]

For pre-moulded, reusable, filtered and custom products, use the supplied fitting method; not all are rolled. The general principles remain clean hands, the correct size/orientation, a complete comfortable seal and uninterrupted wear in the noisy area.[S3][S6][S9]

### Stop rules for fitting

- Do not force a plug deeper through pain or marked discomfort.
- Stop inserting earplugs if there is ear pain, discharge, bleeding, a suspected eardrum injury or a foreign object, and seek the appropriate clinical advice below. NHS advises not putting objects or unprescribed drops into an ear with a possible perforated eardrum.[S12][S13]
- A medical condition can make an earplug unsuitable; HSE includes wearer medical issues in protector selection.[S9]
- Earplug use and water can irritate the ear canal and contribute to outer-ear infection in some people. New pain, itching, swelling, discharge or hearing change should not be dismissed as “just the plug”.[S16]

## 5. Safe reuse, cleaning and storage

The safe rule is to follow the classification and instructions for the **exact product**:[S6][S8][S9]

- **Disposable means one use.** HSE’s detailed guidance says disposable earplugs should only be used once.[S9]
- **Reusable does not mean indefinitely reusable.** Clean reusable plugs regularly and inspect for damage or degradation; replace them when damaged, degraded, dirty beyond cleaning, or no longer fitting/sealing correctly.[S6][S9]
- Use only the manufacturer’s cleaning method. Materials and acoustic filters differ, so a public page should not prescribe alcohol, boiling, detergents, submersion or removal of filters as universal methods.
- After cleaning, follow instructions for drying/reassembly and store the plugs in the clean container or environment specified by the manufacturer.[S6][S9]
- Use clean hands whenever fitting. Compressible plugs should remain soft, pliable and clean.[S6][S8]
- Never share earplugs. HSE recommends reusable earplugs be personal issue and says people should not pass plugs between one another.[S8][S9]
- Carry a spare pair so a dropped, contaminated, lost or damaged plug does not lead to unprotected exposure. This is a practical festival adaptation; it is not a claim that all venues provide replacements.

RNID’s festival article says most festivals, gigs and clubs offer free plugs behind a bar or information point.[S4] That availability is not guaranteed. Safer wording is: **“Bring your own and a spare. If you have none, ask an official information, welfare, bar or medical point whether approved hearing-protection plugs are available.”**

## 6. Limits of consumer attenuation ratings

### What SNR means—and does not mean

SNR (single number rating) condenses a protector’s laboratory attenuation data into one figure. Packaging may also show attenuation at different frequency bands; protection is not identical at every pitch.[S3][S6][S9]

Do not tell an attendee to calculate:

> phone reading in dB(A) − package SNR = personal level at the eardrum

That shortcut is unsound because:[S3][S7][S9]

1. HSE’s SNR method uses a **C-weighted** measured sound level and a defined calculation, not arbitrary subtraction from an A-weighted phone display.
2. Manufacturer data come from standardised tests under ideal conditions with new, correctly fitted protectors.
3. Real-world fit, ear-canal anatomy, loosening, incorrect insertion and product condition reduce protection.
4. HSE applies a 4 dB real-world allowance **after** a standardised calculation for occupational selection. That is a population-oriented planning adjustment, not proof that every wearer will be within 4 dB of the label.
5. SNR/HML methods can be less accurate than octave-band analysis when noise is dominated by particular frequencies, especially low frequencies.[S6][S9]
6. A sound-level app measures at the phone’s microphone and position, not under the individual’s plug. WHO’s formal venue standard requires calibrated monitoring at a defined reference position.[S2]
7. Removing plugs for even a few minutes in a loud area substantially lowers overall protection.[S6][S8]

A rating is therefore useful for comparing and selecting certified hearing protectors, but it does not measure the actual seal, personal attenuation, exposure accumulated across the festival, or individual biological risk. Filtered plugs may deliberately trade some attenuation for more even sound quality; compare the exact product’s tested data rather than marketing language.[S3][S9]

Also avoid overprotection in contexts where hearing speech, alarms, vehicles or safety instructions is essential. HSE cautions that reducing level at the ear below 70 dB can impair communication and warning-signal awareness.[S6] This is workplace selection guidance, not a reason to remove earplugs beside a stage; move to a quieter area for conversation or instructions.

## 7. Warning signs and care routes

A festival page should separate ordinary follow-up, urgent help and emergencies.

| Symptom or situation | Recommended UK route | Source basis |
|---|---|---|
| Ringing/buzzing or muffled hearing after loud music | Leave the loud area, avoid further loud exposure and monitor. If ringing is regular/constant, persists, worsens or is bothersome, arrange a GP assessment/hearing check. Do not describe post-event ringing as harmless even if it fades. | WHO says concert-related tinnitus/muffling can be transient but persistent tinnitus can indicate damage; NHS advises GP review for regular/constant, worsening or bothersome tinnitus.[S1][S11] |
| Tinnitus beating with the pulse | Urgent GP appointment. | NHS tinnitus guidance.[S11] |
| Sudden hearing loss in one or both ears, or hearing worsening over days/weeks | Do not wait until after the festival. Seek an urgent GP appointment or NHS 111. NICE’s clinical pathway says unexplained loss developing over 3 days or less within the past 30 days should be seen by ENT or an emergency department within 24 hours. | NHS hearing-loss guidance and NICE NG98.[S10][S14] |
| Tinnitus **with** sudden hearing loss, facial weakness or vertigo; or tinnitus after a head injury | Go to A&E or call 999. Do not drive yourself. | NHS tinnitus guidance.[S11] |
| Hearing loss together with earache or discharge | Urgent GP appointment or NHS 111; it may need prompt treatment. Stop inserting earplugs or other objects until assessed. | NHS hearing-loss and perforated-eardrum guidance.[S10][S13] |
| Ear pain with fluid/discharge, hearing change, swelling around the ear, high temperature or feeling generally unwell | Urgent GP appointment or NHS 111. Earache lasting more than 2–3 days also needs this route. At a festival, onsite medical staff can help initiate care but do not replace NHS escalation. | NHS earache and ear-infection guidance.[S12][S16] |
| Clear fluid, blood or pus after a loud noise, especially with sudden pain, tinnitus, dizziness or hearing loss | Treat as possible eardrum injury: urgent GP/NHS 111 for sudden loss or loss with pain/discharge; keep the ear dry and put nothing inside unless a clinician directs it. | NHS perforated-eardrum guidance.[S13] |

If symptoms are severe or the person is unsure which route applies, onsite festival medical staff and NHS 111 can triage. An online hearing check may be useful for general screening but must not delay emergency or urgent care for sudden loss or red flags.[S10][S14]

### Why sudden loss should not be minimised

A person may call sudden hearing loss “muffling”, “blocked ears” or assume it is temporary after a set. A public page should say: **if hearing suddenly drops in either ear, especially if one-sided or accompanied by tinnitus, dizziness or facial weakness, act now rather than sleeping on it.** NICE’s 24-hour pathway exists because some causes need rapid specialist assessment.[S14][S15]

## 8. Recommended short-form copy for a festival page

### Core prevention panel

> **Protect your hearing at every loud stage**
>
> - Wear hearing-protection earplugs before entering the loud area; keep them in while it stays loud.
> - Move well away from speakers and amplifiers. If you must raise your voice to be heard at arm’s length, it is too loud.
> - Take at least 10 minutes in a genuinely quiet area every hour. Longer or more frequent quiet breaks are sensible after louder exposure.
> - Foam plugs can work well when fully fitted. Filtered/music plugs may preserve sound quality better, but no style works without a good seal and enough tested attenuation.
> - Bring a spare pair. Never share plugs, and reuse only products marked reusable.[S1][S3][S4][S8][S9]

### Foam fitting panel

> **Foam plug: roll, pull, insert, hold**
>
> 1. Clean your hands and roll the foam narrow.
> 2. With the other hand, pull the ear up and back.
> 3. Insert to a comfortable depth into the canal—not just across the opening.
> 4. Hold until it expands securely. Fit both ears.
> 5. If it loosens, go somewhere quiet and refit or replace it.[S1][S8][S9]

### Red-flag panel

> **Get help**
>
> - **Sudden hearing loss:** urgent GP or NHS 111 now; it may need specialist assessment within 24 hours.
> - **Tinnitus plus sudden hearing loss, facial weakness or vertigo, or tinnitus after a head injury:** A&E or 999.
> - **Hearing loss with ear pain or discharge:** urgent GP or NHS 111.
> - **Regular/constant, worsening or troubling tinnitus:** arrange a GP appointment.[S10][S11][S14]

## 9. Claims to avoid or qualify

- **“Under 85 dB is safe.”** HSE’s 85 dB(A) value is an occupational action threshold, not a public safe/no-safe boundary.[S5][S9]
- **“The venue complies, so your hearing is safe.”** WHO’s 100 dB LAeq,15min is an upper reference-position limit and cannot remove all individual risk.[S2]
- **“These plugs reduce the stage from 100 dB to 75 dB.”** Package SNR does not directly establish an individual’s at-ear level.[S7][S9]
- **“Filtered plugs block harmful frequencies but let safe sound through.”** Filters shape attenuation; they do not identify and remove only “harmful” sound.
- **“Musician plugs are always better than foam.”** They often preserve sound quality better, while actual protection depends on tested attenuation and fit.[S3][S4]
- **“Ringing the next morning is normal.”** It is common after loud sound but indicates excessive exposure and may be temporary or persistent.[S1]
- **“Five/ten minutes’ rest resets your ears.”** Breaks reduce dose; they do not erase prior exposure or guarantee recovery.[S1]
- **“Wash and reuse any foam plug.”** Disposable plugs are one-use; only clean/reuse products according to their designation and instructions.[S9]
- **“Most venues definitely have free plugs.”** Availability varies; advise bringing plugs and asking official staff if needed.[S4]

## Limitations and evidence gaps

- WHO’s exposure tables and venue standard are population-level risk-management tools. They cannot predict whether a particular person, set or festival weekend will cause injury.
- RNID’s general hearing-protection page is practical charity guidance, not a comparative clinical trial of foam versus filtered plugs. Its festival article includes a supplier donation relationship; brand claims were excluded.
- HSE’s fit, SNR and derating materials were written for workplace risk management. They are valuable for explaining protector mechanics and rating limitations but do not place attendee exposure under workplace law.
- No universal cleaning recipe is safe across foam, silicone, rubber, filters and custom moulds. Product-specific instructions control.
- Phone sound-level apps may support awareness, as WHO notes, but cannot replace calibrated venue monitoring or measure personal attenuation under an earplug.
- Symptoms after loud sound can have several causes. A public page should route care rather than diagnose NIHL, infection, wax or eardrum injury.
